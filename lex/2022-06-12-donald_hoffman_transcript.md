### Case Against Reality

**Lex:** In your book, *"The Case Against Reality, Why Evolution Hid the Truth From Our Eyes,"* you make the bold claim that the world we see with our eyes is not real. It's not even an abstraction of objective reality. It is completely detached from objective reality. Can you explain this idea?

**Donald Hoffman:** This is a theorem from evolution by natural selection. The technical question that I and my team asked was, what is the probability that natural selection would shape sensory systems to see true properties of objective reality? And to our surprise, we found that the answer is precisely zero — except for one kind of structure. For any generic structure that you might think the world might have — a total order, a topology, a metric — the probability is precisely zero that natural selection would shape any sensory system of any organism to see any aspect of objective reality.

So in that sense, what we're seeing is what we need to see to stay alive long enough to reproduce. We're seeing what we need to guide adaptive behavior. Full stop.

**Lex:** So the evolutionary process does not maximize for truth — it maximizes for fitness. Fitness beats truth. And fitness does not have to be connected to truth.

**Donald Hoffman:** That's exactly right. Most of us intuitively think that surely the way that evolution will make our senses more fit is to make them tell us more truths. That's the standard view, and it was the view I took. But we don't have to just wave our hands. Evolution by natural selection is a mathematically precise theory. John Maynard Smith in the seventies created evolutionary game theory, and we have evolutionary graph theory, and even genetic algorithms that we can use to study this. It's a matter of theorem and proof.

**Lex:** The thing we see with our eyes is not some kind of limited window into reality. It is completely detached from reality.

**Donald Hoffman:** It's a real experience, and it's a real adaptive set of perceptions. But we've assumed that there's a pretty tight relationship between our perceptions and reality. If I look up and see the moon, then there is something that exists in space and time that matches what I perceive. And all I'm saying is that if you take evolution by natural selection seriously, then that is precluded. Our perceptions are there to guide adaptive behavior, full stop. They're not there to show you the truth. In fact, they're there to hide the truth because the truth is too complicated.

It's just like if you're trying to use your laptop to write an email. What you're doing is toggling voltages in the computer, but good luck trying to do it that way. The reason why we have a user interface is because we don't want to know that truth — the diodes and resistors and all that terrible hardware. If you had to know all that truth, your friends wouldn't hear from you.

What evolution gave us was perceptions that guide adaptive behavior, and part of that process means hiding the truth and giving you eye candy.

### Spacetime Is Doomed

**Lex:** When you say spacetime is doomed, is it the space, is it the time, is it the very hardcoded specification of four dimensions?

**Donald Hoffman:** This is not my quote. This is from Nima Arkani-Hamed at the Institute for Advanced Study at Princeton. Ed Witten, David Gross — Nobel Prize winner. The physicists are saying this.

They're saying that both space and time — their union into spacetime as Einstein's theory — that's doomed. And they're basically saying that even quantum theory, Hilbert spaces will not be fundamental either. So what they're looking for is some new mathematical structures beyond spacetime.

They found something called the Amplituhedron — this is Nima and his colleagues — the cosmological polytopes. These polyhedra in multidimensions code for the scattering amplitudes of processes in the Large Hadron Collider. They're finding that if they let go of spacetime completely, they're finding new ways of computing these scattering amplitudes that turn literally billions of terms into one term.

When you do it in space and time, it's the wrong framework — it's just a user interface. From the evolutionary point of view, it's just a user interface, not a deep insight into the nature of reality.

The Feynman approach to scattering amplitudes is trying to enforce two critical properties of spacetime: locality and unitarity. When you enforce those, you get all these loops and multiple different levels of loops. But when you do it outside of spacetime, you don't have the notion of unitarity, you don't have the notion of locality. You have something deeper, and it's capturing symmetries that are actually true of the data.

So what we're finding is there's this whole new world now beyond spacetime that is making explicit symmetries that are true of the data that cannot be seen in spacetime. That is turning the computations from billions of terms to one or two or a handful of terms.

### Reductionism Is Dead

**Donald Hoffman:** Reductionism is in fact dead, as is spacetime. The ontology of spacetime and its contents, and the methodology of reductionism — which is that as we go to smaller scales in spacetime, we will find more and more fundamental laws — has been very useful for centuries. But now we realize that that's over.

**Lex:** What exactly is reductionism?

**Donald Hoffman:** The reductionist explanation says: I will start with things that are smaller in spacetime and therefore more fundamental, and where the laws are more fundamental. We go to just smaller and smaller scales.

Whereas in science more generally, like when Einstein did the special theory of relativity, he said: let me have a couple postulates. I will assume that the speed of light is universal for all observers in uniform motion. That's not reductionist. You're not going to smaller and smaller scales. You're coming up with deep, deep principles.

Reductionism has been a particularly useful kind of scientific explanation — for example, in thermodynamics, where the notion of heat could be reduced to molecules and atoms bouncing around. But we know now precisely where that has to stop, at 10 to the minus 33 centimeters and 10 to the minus 43 seconds.

### Evolutionary Game Theory

**Donald Hoffman:** My graduate student Justin Mark did simulations using genetic algorithms. We started with random organisms, random sensory genetics and random actions. The first generation was unbelievably bad. But we could then just look at how the genes evolved.

What he found was that you never even saw the truth organisms come on the stage. If they came, they were gone in one generation. The ones that were just tracking the fitness payoffs were far more fit than the truth seekers.

But my attitude as a scientist is: I don't believe any of our theories. I take them very, very seriously. I study them, I look at their implications, but none of them are the gospel. They're just the latest ideas that we have.

In 1890, a lot of physicists thought we'd arrived. They were discouraging bright young students from going into physics, because it was all done. And that's precisely the wrong attitude. Forever, this is the wrong attitude. A century from now, they'll be looking at us and laughing at what we didn't know.

### Consciousness

**Donald Hoffman:** Right now, all of my brilliant colleagues — well, 99% of them — are assuming spacetime is fundamental. They're assuming that particles are fundamental — quarks, gluons, leptons. Elements, atoms are fundamental. And that therefore neurons and brains are part of objective reality. And that somehow when you get matter that's complicated enough, it will somehow generate conscious experiences.

All of the work that's being done on consciousness and its relationship to the brain has all assumed something that our best theories are telling us is doomed: spacetime.

I would love to find a theory of consciousness in which the dynamics of consciousness itself will give rise to the geometry that the physicists are finding beyond spacetime. If we can do that, then we'd have a completely different way of looking at how consciousness is related to what we call the brain or the physical world more generally.

**Lex:** Is this paralyzing? Because how do we know we're not completely adrift, out to sea, lost forever? If we can never truly, deeply introspect to the bottom — if it's always just turtles on top of turtles infinitely — isn't that paralyzing for the scientific mind?

**Donald Hoffman:** One answer that is interesting to explore is something that spiritual traditions have said for thousands of years — that space and time aren't fundamental. They're maya, they're illusion. But if you look inside, if you introspect and let go of all of your particular perceptions, you will come to something that's beyond conceptual thought. And that is, they claim, being in contact with the deep ground of being that transcends any particular conceptual understanding.

If that is correct, then it would be the case that as scientists, because we also are in touch with this ground of being, we would then not be able to conceptually understand ourselves all the way. But we could know ourselves just by being ourselves.
