### Are We Living in a Dream?

**Lex:** You are a writer and a director that has explored deep philosophical ideas. One of the ideas is: are we living inside a dream or a simulation? What's your own sense — are we living inside a dream?

**Alex Garland:** Well, I think it depends what you mean. Do I think we're living in something which is exactly like a dream state? No. But I think our relationship with what's real is mediated by our senses in a way that doesn't necessarily reflect objective reality.

Part of it is that what our senses give us is a reconstruction. The brain is not a passive receiver — the brain is an active participant in constructing the world around us. And the question is: how much of that is objective reality, and how much of it is a kind of subjective, externally generated hallucination?

**Lex:** So there's a control system that's building a representation of reality.

**Alex:** Yes. And that representation is successful in the sense that it allows us to navigate the world. But the question is whether it's the same as the world.

---

### Aliens

**Lex:** You've explored the idea of alien life in *Annihilation*. What do you think — are there aliens out there?

**Alex:** I think statistically, given the sheer number of planets in the universe, it seems more likely than not that there's life elsewhere. But the question is what form that life takes. It might be so different from us that we wouldn't even recognize it as life.

The alien in *Annihilation* is intentionally not a little green man. It's something completely outside our framework of understanding — it's not hostile, it's not friendly, it just is. It's a process. That's what fascinates me: the idea of encountering something so alien that we have no frame of reference for it.

---

### Science Fiction: Imagination Becoming Reality

**Lex:** Science fiction has a way of predicting or inspiring the future. Do you think about that when you write?

**Alex:** I think science fiction is not really about predicting the future. It's about the present. You're using imagined futures to talk about what's happening right now. *Ex Machina* wasn't really about AI in the future — it was about power, control, gender dynamics, and human relationships in the present.

That said, there's an interesting feedback loop. Sometimes science fiction does inspire people to create the things they've seen in fiction. The flip phone was inspired by Star Trek communicators. But that's not why you write it. You write it because you're interested in ideas, and science fiction gives you a framework to explore those ideas in a slightly abstracted way.

---

### Artificial Intelligence

**Lex:** *Ex Machina* is, to me, one of the greatest films about AI. The central question is: can a machine be conscious? Where do you land on that?

**Alex:** I think consciousness is a property of sufficiently complex information processing systems. That's my hunch. I don't think there's anything magical about biological neurons that can't be replicated in other substrates.

But the more interesting question — and this is really what *Ex Machina* is about — is not whether a machine can be conscious, but whether we would recognize that consciousness if we saw it. And whether we would treat it with humanity.

The Turing test in *Ex Machina* is not really about whether Ava can pass as human. It's about whether Nathan can recognize her consciousness even though he knows she's a machine. And he fails — not because she tricks him, but because of his own limitations.

### Devs and Silicon Valley

**Lex:** Your new series *Devs* is set in Silicon Valley. What drew you to that world?

**Alex:** Silicon Valley is fascinating because it presents itself as this kind of utopian, world-changing force for good. And in some ways it is. But there's also a darkness there — a kind of messianic belief in technology that can be quite terrifying.

*Devs* is about a quantum computing company. The central premise is that if you have a sufficiently powerful quantum computer, you could potentially predict the future or see into the past — because at the quantum level, determinism is still very much in play in certain interpretations.

The question becomes: if you could see everything that's going to happen, what does that do to free will? And if you're a tech company with that kind of power, what do you do with it?

**Lex:** In Silicon Valley, there's this tension between building things that genuinely help people and a kind of messianic egotism.

**Alex:** Exactly. And I think that's partly what I'm exploring. The people in *Devs* are not villains in the conventional sense. They genuinely believe they're doing something good. That's what makes it interesting — and more realistic.

### Ex Machina and 2001: A Space Odyssey

**Lex:** *2001: A Space Odyssey* is a film that seems to have influenced you. What is it about that film?

**Alex:** *2001* is extraordinary because it operates on a completely different level from most films. It's almost like a piece of music or a poem. It doesn't explain itself — it trusts the audience to engage with it on its own terms.

The way Kubrick uses imagery, sound, and silence to create meaning is something I think about a lot. There's a confidence in *2001* — a willingness to be ambiguous and to let the audience sit with that ambiguity.

In *Ex Machina*, I was trying to do something similar in a different register. Not the same visual language, but the same trust in the audience. The ending is ambiguous — you have to decide what it means.

---

### Lone Genius

**Lex:** You often explore the figure of the lone genius — Nathan in *Ex Machina*, the character of Forest in *Devs*. What draws you to that archetype?

**Alex:** I'm not sure I'm drawn to it as much as I'm interested in the consequences of it. The lone genius is a myth, first of all. Almost all significant advances are the result of collective effort. The myth of the lone genius is partly a media creation and partly a kind of ego trip.

But what happens when someone believes their own myth? That's what interests me. Nathan in *Ex Machina* is a genius, but he's also a monster. His genius doesn't excuse his behavior — it enables it.

### Drawing Inspiration from Elon Musk

**Lex:** You've mentioned Elon Musk as someone you find interesting. What is it about him?

**Alex:** Elon Musk is interesting because he seems to genuinely be trying to do things that are good for humanity — electric cars, space exploration, neural interfaces. But there's also this ego, this self-mythologizing. And you can't quite separate the two.

What fascinates me is the question: what happens when someone with that kind of vision also has that kind of ego? What does that combination produce?

### Space Travel

**Lex:** Is space travel important for the future of humanity?

**Alex:** I think there's an argument that as a species we should not put all our eggs in one basket. If something happens to Earth — an asteroid, climate catastrophe, whatever — having a self-sustaining colony elsewhere is a kind of insurance policy.

But there's also a deeper reason. Exploration is part of what we are. It's part of what consciousness does — it explores. Sending people to Mars or beyond is an expression of something fundamentally human.

### Free Will

**Lex:** Do we have free will?

**Alex:** I don't know. *Devs* is an exploration of that question. If the universe is deterministic at the quantum level — and there are interpretations of quantum mechanics that suggest it might be, that the wave function collapse is deterministic — then free will is an illusion. Everything you do was predetermined from the moment of the Big Bang.

But even if it is an illusion, it's an incredibly powerful and convincing illusion. We live as if we have free will. And maybe that's enough.

What's interesting to me is not the answer to the question, but what happens when people are confronted with the possibility that free will doesn't exist. How do they behave? What does it do to their sense of responsibility, morality, meaning?

---

### Devs and the Poetry of Science

**Lex:** There's a poetic quality to the way you depict science. Where does that come from?

**Alex:** Science, at its best, is poetic. The ideas are beautiful. Quantum mechanics, the double-slit experiment, entanglement — these are mind-bending, beautiful ideas. They're not just technical achievements; they're profound statements about the nature of reality.

I think science and art are not in opposition. They're different ways of exploring the same questions: What is the nature of reality? What is consciousness? What does it mean to be human?

In *Devs*, I tried to show the beauty of those questions. The characters are not just technicians — they're philosophers, in a way. They're grappling with the biggest questions there are.

### What Will You Be Remembered For?

**Lex:** What do you hope people will remember you for?

**Alex:** I don't know. I'm not sure I think in those terms. I make things because I'm interested in the ideas. If the things I make spark conversations or make people think about these questions, that's great.

But I'm also aware that whatever I do will be reinterpreted, misunderstood, and forgotten. And that's fine. The work exists, and then it doesn't. What matters is the process of making it — the exploration.
